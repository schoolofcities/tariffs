About the job impact scenarios

By Yihoi Jung, Richard DiFrancesco, Clara Turner, Tara Vinodrai, Karen Chapple
~ First published: November 2, 2026

From our research on direct exposure to Canadian businesses and jobs, we saw the potential effect of U.S. tariffs through business and employee vulnerabilities. However, the question remained as to how this exposure could convert into job losses for specific scenarios in the future.

These scenarios are the extended-effects models we noted in our Section 338 update. While earlier work sought potential direct exposure, these scenarios ask a different question: if a sector undergoes economic turmoil, how many jobs are lost, and where do those losses land?

How the model works

Each scenario begins with an economic shock, whether it be a decline in household consumption, or a drop in exports from a particular sector. This shock is run through a Multiregional Input-Output (MRIO) model covering all of Canada's provinces and territories. The model tracks changes not only within its sector but also in its indirect and induced fields as a ripple effect.

The model returns a dollar impact for every province and industry. Those dollars are converted into jobs using Statistics Canada's employment multipliers, and then distributed to local geographies in proportion to how much of that industry's provincial employment each place actually holds. There are two geographies - census subdivisions (CSDs) and aggregate dissemination areas (ADAs). Census subdivisions generally represent municipalities while aggregate dissemination areas are smaller and more uniform in population.

This last step matters for interpretation. The model is provincial; the map is local. An ADA or CSD's figure is its share of a provincial estimate, not an independent measurement of that municipality. Places with a higher share of affected workers have more estimated loss.

Direct, indirect, and induced effects

The map lets you select three effects, individually or in combination. They are additive, and they describe three stages of the same disruption.

Direct effects are job losses in the industries hit by the shock itself such as the sawmill, the parts plant, or the processing facility.

Indirect effects are job losses along the supply chain.

Induced effects are job losses supported by the spending of the workers above.

For most scenarios, these downstream effects are substantially larger than the direct effect alone, so direct exposure generally understates the entire picture.

The scenarios

Household Consumption

Overall household consumption expenditures in Canada decline by 1%. Scotiabank Economics and the Bank of Canada have given their projections of consumption decline, ranging from 0.5% to 2% of GDP consumption in Canada due to the tariffs.

Agri-food & Seafood

Agriculture, agri-food, and seafood exports to the U.S. decline. Although not as directly affected, analysis from Agri-Pulse and Retail Insider show significant declines in this sector in terms of agri-food exports.

Steel & Aluminum

Steel and aluminum manufacturing exports to the U.S. decline. The Financial Accountability Office (FAO) of Ontario reported a potential 137,900 job decrease in Ontario by 2029 under the April 2025 tariff scenario.

Softwood Lumber

Softwood lumber exports to the U.S. decline. The softwood lumber tariffs have affected the industry drastically, leading to declining lumber production and exports as noted by a report from the Royal Bank of Canada and our softwood lumber blog.

Autos & Parts

Automobile and auto-parts exports to the U.S. decline. The FAO noted a potential 20% decrease in U.S. motor vehicles exports and a 30% decrease for motor parts by 2029.

Limitations

These are scenarios, not predictions where each one describes the implication of an assumed shock, not the guarantee that these jobs will be lost. The model assumes the current industry as fixed. It does not capture firms finding new markets, substituting suppliers, relocating production, or any of the adaptive responses businesses and governments have been making. Local CSD and ADA figures are projections of provincial estimates. They are most reliable read as a pattern across places, and least reliable read as a precise count for a single place. A small number of industries have no concordance between the input-output classification and the employment data, so a share of modelled impact cannot be placed geographically.
